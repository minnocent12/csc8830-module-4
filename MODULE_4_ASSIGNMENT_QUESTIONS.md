# Evaluation and Validation

Both Question 1 and Question 2 should include experimental validation.

The comparison should not rely solely on visual inspection.

Where ground-truth or SAM2 masks are available, useful evaluation metrics may include:

---

## Intersection over Union — IoU

Let:

* \(A\) = region generated by the OpenCV implementation
* \(B\) = comparison/reference segmentation

Then:

$$
IoU =
\frac{|A \cap B|}
{|A \cup B|}
$$

A larger IoU indicates stronger agreement between the two segmentation regions.

---

## Dice Similarity Coefficient

The Dice coefficient can be calculated as:

$$
Dice =
\frac{2|A \cap B|}
{|A| + |B|}
$$

A Dice score closer to:

$$
1
$$

indicates stronger similarity.

---

## Precision

$$
Precision =
\frac{TP}
{TP+FP}
$$

where:

* \(TP\) = true-positive pixels
* \(FP\) = false-positive pixels

---

## Recall

$$
Recall =
\frac{TP}
{TP+FN}
$$

where:

* \(FN\) = false-negative pixels

## Edge Detection and Region Segmentation Using the Fourier Domain

Explain mathematically how **edge detection** and **region segmentation** can be achieved using Fourier or frequency-domain analysis.

Your explanation should include mathematical derivations and clearly connect spatial-domain image characteristics with their frequency-domain representation.

---

# Part A — Fourier Representation of an Image

Let an image be represented in the spatial domain as:

$$
f(x,y)
$$

Its two-dimensional Fourier transform is:

$$
F(u,v)
=
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
f(x,y)
e^{-j2\pi(ux+vy)}
dx\,dy
$$

For a digital image, use the corresponding discrete two-dimensional Fourier transform.

The inverse Fourier transform reconstructs the image:

$$
f(x,y)
=
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{F(u,v)\}
$$

Explain the meaning of:

* Low spatial frequencies
* High spatial frequencies
* Frequency magnitude
* Frequency phase

---

# Part B — Relationship Between Edges and High Frequencies

Edges represent rapid changes in image intensity.

Rapid intensity changes correspond primarily to **high-frequency components** in the Fourier domain.

Therefore, edge detection can be performed by emphasizing high frequencies while reducing low-frequency components.

A frequency-domain filtering operation can be written as:

$$
G(u,v)
=
H(u,v)F(u,v)
$$

where:

* \(F(u,v)\) is the Fourier transform of the input image
* \(H(u,v)\) is a frequency-domain filter
* \(G(u,v)\) is the filtered spectrum

The resulting spatial-domain image is:

$$
g(x,y)
=
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{G(u,v)\}
$$

---

# Part C — High-Pass Filtering for Edge Detection

A high-pass filter suppresses low frequencies and preserves or enhances high frequencies.

Conceptually:

$$
H_{HP}(u,v)
=
1-H_{LP}(u,v)
$$

where:

* \(H_{HP}\) is a high-pass filter
* \(H_{LP}\) is the corresponding low-pass filter

The filtered spectrum becomes:

$$
G(u,v)
=
H_{HP}(u,v)F(u,v)
$$

Applying the inverse Fourier transform gives:

$$
g(x,y)
=
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
\left\{
H_{HP}(u,v)F(u,v)
\right\}
$$

The result emphasizes rapid image variations, including object boundaries and edges.

---

# Part D — Derivative Property of the Fourier Transform

Use the derivative property of the Fourier transform to explain why edge operators correspond to high-frequency enhancement.

For a one-dimensional signal:

$$
\mathcal{F}
\left\{
\frac{df(x)}{dx}
\right\}
=
j2\pi uF(u)
$$

For a two-dimensional image:

$$
\mathcal{F}
\left\{
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}
\right\}
=
j2\pi uF(u,v)
$$

and:

$$
\mathcal{F}
\left\{
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}
\right\}
=
j2\pi vF(u,v)
$$

Because differentiation multiplies the spectrum by frequency, higher frequencies are amplified.

This provides the mathematical connection between:

* Image gradients
* Edge detection
* Frequency-domain filtering

---

# Part E — Laplacian in the Frequency Domain

The spatial Laplacian is:

$$
\nabla^2 f
=
\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}
+
\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2}
$$

Using Fourier-transform properties:

$$
\mathcal{F}
\{
\nabla^2 f
\}
=
-4\pi^2
(u^2+v^2)
F(u,v)
$$

Therefore, the corresponding frequency-domain filter is:

$$
H(u,v)
=
-4\pi^2(u^2+v^2)
$$

This demonstrates mathematically why the Laplacian emphasizes high-frequency components and can be used to detect edges.

---

# Part F — Fourier-Domain Region Segmentation

Region segmentation aims to separate areas of an image based on properties such as:

* Intensity
* Texture
* Spatial-frequency content
* Repeating patterns

Different regions may produce different frequency characteristics.

For example:

* Smooth regions generally contain more low-frequency information.
* Highly textured regions generally contain more high-frequency information.
* Repeating structures may produce recognizable peaks in the frequency spectrum.

---

# Frequency-Based Region Analysis

A general frequency-domain segmentation procedure may involve:

1. Computing the Fourier transform:

$$
F(u,v)=\mathcal{F}\{f(x,y)\}
$$

2. Applying a frequency-selective filter:

$$
G(u,v)=H(u,v)F(u,v)
$$

3. Computing the inverse transform:

$$
g(x,y)
=
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{G(u,v)\}
$$

4. Using the filtered image to identify regions with particular frequency characteristics.

5. Applying thresholding or region analysis to obtain the final segmentation.

---

# Local Frequency Analysis

Because the standard Fourier transform represents global frequency information, explain why segmentation may require analysis over smaller spatial regions.

A useful conceptual approach is to divide an image into local windows:

$$
f_k(x,y)
$$

and compute:

$$
F_k(u,v)
=
\mathcal{F}
\{
f_k(x,y)
\}
$$

for each region \(k\).

Frequency features can then be extracted from each local spectrum and used to classify or separate image regions.

This establishes a relationship between:

* Local texture
* Frequency content
* Region segmentation

without requiring machine-learning methods.
